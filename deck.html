Resolution order for every field is ttm_quarterly → statement_fy → info → derived, and the winning source is recorded. Provenance is an output, not a debugging aid — a reviewer can see which figures came from filed statements and which the tool computed itself.

Nothing is silently imputed. Where a value is estimated it is tagged derived and surfaced in the provenance table.

03 · What broke

Four things broke. The tool found all four.

1

Fiserv — a $25bn company with no financials recovered

The summary endpoint returned a market cap but no revenue, no EBITDA, no industry. The filed-statements endpoint had all of it. A naive info["totalRevenue"] pipeline drops a mega-cap silently.

2

MeridianLink — the ticker is dead removed

Returned 404 · Quote not found. Taken private by Centerbridge. Caught by probing the universe empirically before building around it.

3

i3 Verticals — no longer a payments company excluded

Revenue read $218M against a remembered scale near $385M. The filed business description resolved it: it now sells software to courts and municipalities.

4

Open Lending — +288% growth that isn’t growth corrected

Revenue ran $179.6M → $117.5M → $24.0M → $93.2M. The 2024 collapse is a profit-share write-down; the bounce is recovery, not expansion. Trend substituted: −19.6%.

04 · Handling policy

Exclude the metric, not the company

Free data is wrong in three different ways, and they need three different responses. Collapsing them into “drop the row” destroys information.

Tier 1 · Flag, keep in the median

The value is real, the pedigree is worth knowing. EV_DERIVED — enterprise value we computed because the API withheld it.

Tier 2 · Flag, drop that metric, keep the row

A negative EV/EBITDA is not a cheap multiple, it is a category error, and it corrupts the median for everyone else. But the same company’s EV/Revenue is fine. Alkami keeps its revenue multiple and loses its EBITDA multiple.

Tier 3 · Exclude the company, in writing

Reserved for business-model mismatch — never for numbers being inconvenient. One name qualifies: i3 Verticals.

Tier 2 is where most of the value is. Dropping a whole row because one field is unusable is the common shortcut, and it quietly shrinks the peer set exactly where the interesting names live.

05 · Self-correction

A flag that fired on 9 of 15 names was the bug

An early run flagged API_MULTIPLE_DISAGREES — our recomputed multiple versus the API’s — on 60% of the universe. A flag that fires that often is either miscalibrated or hiding something.

PYPL   FY_stmt  33,172    info_TTM  34,128    gap  +2.9%
GPN    FY_stmt   7,706    info_TTM  10,207    gap +32.5%
PAYO   FY_stmt     821    info_TTM   1,081    gap +31.7%
MITK   FY_stmt     180    info_TTM     198    gap +10.1%

It was hiding two things. Statement revenue is fiscal year; the API’s summary revenue is TTM — the pipeline was comparing across periods. And underneath that, Global Payments reports on two different bases entirely.

Rebuilding TTM from quarterly filings dropped the flag to near zero — and changed real conclusions. Marqeta’s EBITDA flips sign between the two bases. The fix was not cosmetic.

06 · Gross vs net

Why payments can’t be valued on revenue

Payments companies report revenue gross (including interchange passed through to networks and issuers) or net of it. Both are legitimate. Neither is comparable to the other.

CompanyNet revenueGross revenueGapEV/Revenue
GPN$7,706M$10,231M+32.8%suppressed
PAYO$821M$862M+31.7%*suppressed
PRTH$1,000M — gross-basis reporter—1.6×
 *Gap measured on the fiscal-year basis where the divergence appears. Ranking these three on EV/Revenue would rank them by accounting policy, not by value.

EBITDA sits below those passthroughs and is comparable. So the tool assigns a primary multiple per sub-sector — EV/EBITDA for payments, EV/Revenue for FI software — and suppresses the unreliable one rather than averaging both into a number that means nothing.

08 · Valuation

My first DCF said Priority was worth +584%. So I inverted it.

Two things were wrong. The scenario levers stacked multiplicatively. And a 9.5% WACC with 2.5% terminal growth implies a terminal value near 14.6× FCFF — when the market pays 6–8× EBITDA for these businesses.

A forward DCF on free data produces a number nobody should trust. A reverse DCF produces a falsifiable statement: what must the market believe for today’s price to be right?

10 · The error worth dwelling on

The AI was right. The world moved.

Claim MQ-1 — “Marqeta’s EBITDA is negative.”

True on FY2025: −$19M. The model’s knowledge ends May 2026 and reported that correctly.

TTM EBITDA is +$20.8M. Marqeta crossed into EBITDA-positive territory in the intervening quarters.

Why this one is instructive. The error was invisible on annual data. It only appeared once the pipeline computed TTM from quarterly filings. An AI layer checked against the same stale basis the AI used would have confirmed the error rather than caught it.

Verification only works when the data is fresher than the model.

Two larger errors were caught before modelling began and are written up in full: the i3 Verticals misclassification, and MeridianLink’s dead ticker. Both came from the same habit — probe the universe empirically before designing around it.

12 · Robustness

The weights are arbitrary. So I attacked them.

The obvious challenge to any scoring model is “you picked those weights — what if they were different?” So I ran every plausible combination rather than waiting to be asked.

The gate is doing real work, not decoration.

But it is sensitive to price, and I can prove it. I built this on 20 September and refreshed it on the 26th. In six days Mitek rose 5.6%, nCino fell 7.8%, and the peer median fell from 4.13× to 3.77×. The recommendation changed.

That is not a defect I am confessing — it is a property of comps analysis that most screens hide. A ranking built on relative multiples is a snapshot, and mine is dated and reproducible on purpose.

13 · Recommendation

Pursue nCino, with low conviction. Pass Open Lending, with high conviction.

PURSUE — NCNO · $2.05bn

The best-rounded name inside the mandate: value 0.67, quality 0.75, growth 0.67, and the only company in the universe with zero data-quality flags. Cloud loan origination for banks, sold on multi-year contracts.

But the discount is +1.0%, which is inside the noise of this dataset — and the reverse DCF says the price already assumes +7.4% terminal growth. I am recommending it because it ranks first, not because it is cheap.

PASS — LPRO · $372M

Raw score 0.40 → confidence-adjusted 0.20, the largest gap in the universe. Revenue is substantially an accounting estimate — a profit-share receivable on insured auto loans, revised down hard enough to produce negative revenue quarters.

Headline growth +288%; real trend −19.6%. The price implies +12.6% terminal growth for a shrinking, loss-making business.

The single biggest reason I could be wrong: the mandate and the opportunity are in different places.

The only genuinely cheap business here is Fiserv — a 13% discount, 38.9% margins, the highest raw score in the universe — and my own <$5bn mandate filter excludes it at $24.7bn. So my recommendation is the best name in a sub-sector where nothing is mispriced, while the actual opportunity sits outside my constraint.

If I am wrong, it is because I answered the question I set myself instead of saying the honest thing: on this data, today, the right action is to widen the universe rather than force a pick.

14 · Next

With three more days

  • SEC EDGAR XBRL as an independent second source. Every material figure reconciled against the filing, with a variance report. This removes the single largest weakness — everything currently rests on one free API.
  • Widen the universe. The screen is telling me 15 names is too few to find mispricing; the multiples cluster between 3.17× and 4.10×. Forty names would give the medians something to discriminate against.
  • Segment-level data. Priority’s Treasury business and Mitek’s identity business are the theses, and consolidated figures hide both.
  • Rate sensitivity as a first-class input. Payoneer and Priority both earn float income on customer balances. Neither is modelled as rate-exposed, and for Payoneer that is most of the story.
  • Backtest the confidence gate. Run the screen on data as of 12 months ago and measure whether the gate actually improved forward returns. Right now it is principled but unvalidated.

The honest summary: this prototype is good at deciding which numbers not to trust, and it does not yet know anything the filings would tell it. Those are the same problem from two directions.
