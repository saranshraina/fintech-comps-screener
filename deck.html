01 · Segmentation

Split by revenue mechanics, not by industry label

Merchant payments & processing

Revenue = volume × take rate. Mature, EBITDA-positive, cash-generative, macro-sensitive.

PYPL · GPN · FISV · EVTC · PAYO · MQ · PRTH · PMTS

Financial-institution software

Revenue = multi-year recurring contracts sold to banks and credit unions. High visibility, heavy reinvestment, often GAAP-unprofitable.

JKHY · QTWO · NCNO · ALKT · MITK · LPRO · IIIV

Primary multiple · EV/Revenue

Why the labels are useless here. The API classifies PayPal as “Credit Services”, Priority Technology as “Software – Infrastructure”, and CPI Card Group as “Credit Services”. Those cut straight across the economic distinction that actually drives the multiple, so segmentation is manual and business-model-based.

Resolution order for every field is ttm_quarterly → statement_fy → info → derived, and the source that won is recorded. Provenance is an output, not a debugging aid: a reviewer can see which figures came from filed statements and which the tool computed itself.

Nothing is silently imputed. Where a value is estimated it is tagged derived and surfaced in the provenance table.

03 · Messy data

Four things broke. The tool found all four.

1

Fiserv — a $25bn company with no financials recovered

The summary endpoint returned a market cap but no revenue, no EBITDA, no industry. The filed-statements endpoint had all of it, correctly. A naive info["totalRevenue"] pipeline drops a mega-cap silently. Hence the fallback chain.

2

MeridianLink — the ticker is dead removed

Returned 404 · Quote not found. Taken private by Centerbridge. Caught by probing the universe empirically before building around it — a dead ticker found late is expensive.

3

i3 Verticals — no longer a payments company excluded

Revenue read $213M against a remembered scale near $385M. The filed business description resolved it: it now sells software to courts and municipalities. It divested merchant services.

4

Open Lending — +288% growth that isn’t growth corrected

Revenue ran $179.6M → $117.5M → $24.0M → $93.2M. The 2024 collapse is a profit-share write-down. The bounce off it is recovery, not expansion. Trend substituted: −19.6%.

04 · Handling policy

Exclude the metric, not the company

Free data is wrong in three different ways, and they need three different responses. Collapsing them into “drop the row” destroys information.

Tier 1 · Flag, keep in the median

The value is real, the pedigree is worth knowing. EV_DERIVED — enterprise value we computed because the API withheld it.

Tier 2 · Flag, drop that metric, keep the row

A negative EV/EBITDA is not a cheap multiple, it is a category error, and it corrupts the median for everyone else. But the same company’s EV/Revenue is perfectly good. Alkami keeps its revenue multiple and loses its EBITDA multiple.

Tier 3 · Exclude the company, in writing

Reserved for business-model mismatch — never for numbers being inconvenient. One name qualifies: i3 Verticals.

The distinction matters because Tier 2 is where most of the value is. Dropping a whole row because one field is unusable is the common shortcut, and it quietly shrinks the peer set exactly where the interesting names live.

05 · Self-correction

A flag that fired on 9 of 15 names was the bug

An early run flagged API_MULTIPLE_DISAGREES — our recomputed multiple versus the API’s — on 60% of the universe. A flag that fires that often is either miscalibrated or hiding something.

PYPL   FY_stmt  33,172    info_TTM  34,128    gap  +2.9%
GPN    FY_stmt   7,706    info_TTM  10,207    gap +32.5%
PAYO   FY_stmt     821    info_TTM   1,081    gap +31.7%
MITK   FY_stmt     180    info_TTM     198    gap +10.1%

It was hiding two things. Statement revenue is fiscal year; the API’s summary revenue is TTM — the pipeline was comparing across periods. And underneath that, Global Payments reports on two different bases entirely.

Rebuilding TTM from quarterly filings dropped the flag to near zero — and changed real conclusions. Marqeta’s EBITDA flips sign between the two bases. The fix was not cosmetic.

06 · Gross vs net

Why payments can’t be valued on revenue

Payments companies report revenue gross (including interchange passed through to networks and issuers) or net of it. Both are legitimate. Neither is comparable to the other.

CompanyNet revenueGross revenueGapEV/Revenue
GPN$7,706M$10,207M+32.5%suppressed
PAYO$821M$1,081M+31.7%suppressed
PRTH$1,000M — gross-basis reporter—1.4×
 Ranking these three on EV/Revenue would rank them by accounting policy, not by value.

EBITDA sits below those passthroughs and is comparable. So the tool assigns a primary multiple per sub-sector — EV/EBITDA for payments, EV/Revenue for FI software — and suppresses the unreliable one rather than averaging both into a number that means nothing.

08 · Valuation

My first DCF said Priority was worth +584%. So I inverted it.

Two things were wrong. The scenario levers stacked multiplicatively. And more fundamentally, a 9.5% WACC with 2.5% terminal growth implies a terminal value near 14.6× FCFF — when the market pays 6–8× EBITDA for these businesses.

A forward DCF on free data produces a number nobody should trust. A reverse DCF produces a falsifiable statement: what does the market have to believe for today’s price to be right?

CompanyPriceImplied terminal growthReading
PYPL$52.41−2.2%Priced for perpetual decline

The tool flags its own unreliability. Where the DCF base case diverges from the primary comps multiple by more than 50%, it says so. Where more than 75% of value sits in the terminal year, it labels the output a terminal-value bet rather than a valuation.

10 · The error worth dwelling on

The AI was right. The world moved.

Claim MQ-1 — “Marqeta’s EBITDA is negative.”

True on FY2025: −$19M. The model’s knowledge ends May 2026 and reported that correctly.

TTM EBITDA is +$20.8M. Marqeta crossed into EBITDA-positive territory in the intervening quarters.

Why this one is instructive. The error was invisible on annual data. It only appeared once the pipeline computed TTM from quarterly filings. An AI layer checked against the same stale basis the AI used would have confirmed the error rather than caught it.

Verification only works when the data is fresher than the model.

Two larger errors were caught before modelling began and are written up in full: the i3 Verticals misclassification, and MeridianLink’s dead ticker. Both came from the same habit — probe the universe empirically before designing around it.

11 · Ranking

A company cannot rank highly on numbers we don’t trust

score = (0.35·value + 0.30·quality + 0.20·growth − 0.15·risk) × confidence

12 · Recommendation

Pursue Mitek. Pass Open Lending.

PURSUE — MITK · $755M

3.59× EV/Revenue against a 4.13× peer median — a 13% discount with the highest quality score in its sub-sector (0.92: 27% EBITDA margin, strong cash conversion). The structure is a declining but very high-margin annuity — mobile cheque-deposit software and its patent estate, licensed to most large US banks — funding a growth option in identity verification.

PASS — LPRO · $372M

Raw score 0.387 → confidence-adjusted 0.135, the largest gap in the universe. Revenue is substantially an accounting estimate — a profit-share receivable on insured auto loans, revised down hard enough to produce negative revenue quarters. The headline +288% growth is recovery off that collapse; the real trend is −19.6%.

The single biggest reason I could be wrong: Mitek’s moat and its risk are the same asset.

The thesis rests on the cheque annuity being durable enough to fund the identity transition. If cheque volumes decline faster than modelled, or the patent estate expires or is invalidated, the annuity doesn’t fade — it steps down, and the growth option isn’t yet large enough to absorb it.

My screen cannot see this, because everything it measures is trailing. A 27% trailing margin on a structurally shrinking base looks identical to 27% on a stable one. Mitek has also had delayed filings historically — a governance signal, and a data-integrity risk for a tool that trusts reported figures.

13 · Next

With three more days

  • SEC EDGAR XBRL as an independent second source. Every material figure reconciled against the filing, with a variance report. This removes the single largest weakness — everything currently rests on one free API.
  • Segment-level data. Priority’s Treasury business and Mitek’s identity business are the theses, and consolidated figures hide both.
  • Rate sensitivity as a first-class input. Payoneer and Priority both earn float income on customer balances. Neither is modelled as rate-exposed, and for Payoneer that is most of the story.
  • Retrieval-grounded AI layer with citations. Replace recalled knowledge with retrieved filings, collapsing the staleness window from four months to zero.
  • Backtest the confidence gate. Run the screen on data as of 12 months ago and measure whether the gate actually improved forward returns. Right now it is principled but unvalidated.

The honest summary of this prototype: it is good at deciding which numbers not to trust, and it does not yet know anything the filings would tell it. Those are the same problem from two directions.
