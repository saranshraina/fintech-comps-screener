How I approached it

Approach

Seven stages. Two of them exist only to reject data rather than process it.

StageWhat it doesWhy I built it that way
01 · FetchPulls each company from several endpoints in priority orderOne endpoint silently failed on a $24bn company
02 · CompsRecomputes every multiple myselfI keep the provider's version only to cross-check mine
03 · Quality gate13 flags across three response tiersThis is where most of my effort went
04 · MediansSub-sector benchmarks that honour suppressionA bad metric must not poison the peer set
05 · ValuationThree scenarios, a sensitivity grid, and a reverse DCFMy forward DCF was not credible; see slide 13
06 · AI verification41 machine-checkable claims tested on every runSo "I checked the AI" is an artefact, not a promise
07 · ScreenWeighted score multiplied by a data-confidence factorA company cannot rank on numbers I don't trust
The rule I applied throughout

Every field records where it came from — ttm_quarterly → statement_fy → info → derived. I never impute silently. If I estimated something, it is tagged derived and shows up in the provenance table.

How I used AI to build the tool

Using AI

I wrote almost none of this code by hand. I used Claude to generate each module, then treated everything it produced as a draft to be verified — the same stance I took toward the financial data.

ModuleWhat I asked the model forHow I verified it
src/fetch.pyA resolution chain across four endpoints with provenance recorded per fieldRan it across all 15 tickers and inspected which source won for each field
src/quality.py13 flags with severity levels and metric-level suppressionChecked the firing rate per flag — one fired on 60% of the universe and turned out to be a bug1
src/valuation.pyThree-scenario DCF, sensitivity grid, and a bisection solver for the reverse DCFSanity-checked outputs against the comps; the first version implied +584% upside
src/score.pyPercentile ranking within sub-sector with a confidence multiplierStress-tested 36 weight permutations to see whether the answer was an artefact
src/verify.pyA harness to evaluate assertions against the pulled dataThis module is the verification of the research layer
My working method

Generate, run, inspect the output, and treat anything that looks too clean as suspect. Every module was executed against live data before I trusted it — I never accepted code on the basis that it looked correct.

Five bugs the AI wrote, and how I found them

Using AI

None of these were flagged by the model. Each surfaced only because I ran the code and interrogated the output.

01
A silent name collision with the pandas API
The model named a column flags. Pandas already exposes DataFrame.flags as a property, so reading it raised KeyError: 0 from deep inside pandas rather than from my code. Found by running it — it would never have shown up on inspection.
Crash
02
Comparing fiscal-year revenue against trailing-twelve-month revenue
The generated fetch layer read annual statements while the provider's summary is TTM. The pipeline was silently comparing different periods. Found because a quality flag fired on 9 of 15 names — a rate that told me the flag was either miscalibrated or hiding something. It was hiding this.
Wrong answers
03
A valuation model that stacked its own assumptions
The first DCF multiplied three favourable levers together and returned a +584% bull case for Priority Technology. The arithmetic was correct; the model was unconstrained. Found by sanity-checking the output against the comps table, which said the same company was fairly valued.
Not credible
04
An undeclared dependency that would have crashed the live demo
The dashboard used pandas' background_gradient, which silently requires matplotlib — not installed here. Found by exercising the render path before the demo rather than trusting that the app started. I replaced it with a dependency-free function.
Demo risk
05
A scoring model that penalised the wrong thing
My confidence gate charged Fiserv the same penalty as Open Lending — but Fiserv's problem was that the API broke and my fallback fixed it, while Open Lending's problem is that its revenue figure misleads. Found by reading the ranking and disagreeing with it. Separating the two changed the result.
Wrong ranking

How I decided what to do about each one

Data quality

Bad data is bad in three different ways, and I think they need three different responses. Collapsing them all into "drop the row" throws away good information.

Tier I
Flag it, keep it in the median
The value is correct; its pedigree is worth disclosing. Example: an enterprise value I computed myself because the provider withheld it.
Tier II
Flag it, drop that one metric, keep the company
A negative EV/EBITDA is not a cheap multiple — it is undefined, and letting it into a median corrupts the benchmark for everyone else. But that same company's EV/Revenue is perfectly good. Alkami keeps its revenue multiple and loses its EBITDA multiple.
Tier III
Exclude the company, and write down why
I reserved this for business-model mismatch, never for inconvenient numbers. One company qualified: i3 Verticals.
The principle I would defend

I suppress the metric, not the company. Dropping a whole constituent because one field is unusable shrinks the peer set exactly where the under-covered names sit — which is the part of the universe the screen is supposed to add value in.

When my own flag exposed a fault in my method

Data quality

An early run flagged API_MULTIPLE_DISAGREES on 9 of 15 names. A flag that fires on 60% of a universe is either miscalibrated or concealing something.

CompanyFiled FY revenue ($m)Provider TTM revenue ($m)GapWhat it turned out to be
PYPL33,17234,1282.9%Ordinary period drift
MITK18019810.1%Ordinary period drift
PAYO8211,08131.7%Gross vs. net presentation
GPN7,70610,23132.8%Gross vs. net presentation

The cause

Filed revenue is fiscal year; the provider's summary is trailing twelve months. I was comparing across periods without realising. Underneath that, two companies report on entirely different revenue bases.

What fixing it changed

I rebuilt TTM from quarterly filings. The flag fell to near zero — and real conclusions moved: Marqeta's EBITDA reverses sign between the two bases. This was not a cosmetic fix.

Why I do not value payments companies on revenue

Data quality · Method

Payments companies report revenue gross of interchange passed to networks and issuers, or net of it. Both are legitimate. Neither is comparable to the other.

CompanyNet revenue ($m)Gross revenue ($m)GapEV / RevenueWhat I did
GPN7,70610,23132.8%n.m.Suppressed from the median
PAYO8211,08131.7%n.m.Suppressed from the median
PRTH1,000 — reports on a gross basis—1.6×Retained
Why this drives my whole method

If I ranked these three on EV/Revenue I would be ranking them by accounting policy, not by value. EBITDA sits below those pass-through costs, so it is comparable.

This is why I assign a primary multiple per sub-sector — EV/EBITDA for payments, EV/Revenue for FI software — and suppress the unreliable one rather than averaging both into a number that means nothing.

The one worth dwelling on — MQ-1

The model said Marqeta's EBITDA is negative. On FY2025 that was correct: ($19m). TTM is +$20.8m — it crossed over after the model's cut-off. The error was invisible on annual data and only appeared because I had rebuilt TTM from quarterly filings for an unrelated reason.

Verification only works when your data is fresher than your model.

What I am not claiming

Three of the five failures are not model errors — they were true when written and became false as prices moved. My harness cannot tell those apart, and I do not pretend otherwise.

Roughly 60% of the qualitative layer is not machine-checkable at all. Those claims carry a confidence field and are excluded from my ranking — they inform judgement, they do not score.

What I recommend, why I might be wrong, and what I would do next

Recommendation

Pursue — nCino (NCNO), $1,998m

The best-balanced company inside my mandate: value 0.67, quality 0.75, growth 0.67, and the only name in the universe with no data-quality flags at all. Cloud loan origination sold to banks on multi-year contracts.

My conviction is low and I would rather say so. The discount is 1.0%, inside the noise of this dataset, and my reverse DCF says the price already assumes 7.4% terminal growth.

Pass — Open Lending (LPRO), $372m

Raw score 0.40, confidence-adjusted 0.20 — the widest gap in my universe. Its revenue is substantially an accounting estimate: a profit-share receivable on insured auto loans, revised down hard enough to produce negative revenue quarters. Reported growth +288%; real trend (19.6%). The price implies 12.6% terminal growth for a shrinking, loss-making business.

The single biggest reason I could be wrong

My mandate and the actual opportunity are not in the same place.

The only company here trading at a real discount is Fiserv — 13.0% below the median, 38.9% margins, the highest raw score in my universe — and my own $5,000m filter excludes it. So I am recommending the best name in a sub-sector where nothing is mispriced.

If I am wrong, it is because I answered the question I set myself instead of saying the more useful thing: on this data, the right move is to widen the universe, not to force a pick from fifteen names spanning 3.17× to 4.10×.

If I had three more days

  • SEC EDGAR XBRL as a second, independent source, with a variance report per line item. This removes my single largest weakness — everything currently rests on one free provider.
  • Widen the universe to ~40 names. My medians do not have enough dispersion to discriminate.
  • Segment-level data. Priority's Treasury business and Mitek's identity business are the theses; consolidated figures hide both.
  • Model rate sensitivity properly. Payoneer and Priority both earn income on customer balances and I do not capture that.
  • Back-test the confidence factor on data as at twelve months ago, to find out whether it actually improved forward returns. Right now it is principled but unvalidated.
